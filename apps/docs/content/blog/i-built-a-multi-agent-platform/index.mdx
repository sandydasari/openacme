Two people grab the same ticket. Each does the work, neither knows about the other, and now you've got two half-right answers and a quiet argument about which one counts. Nobody planned that. It just happens the moment two people can act on the same thing without checking with each other first, and every team eventually builds rituals to stop it: standups, locks, the one person who's allowed to push the deploy. None of that is bureaucracy for its own sake. It's the price of letting more than one actor touch the same state.

I built the maximal version of this with language models instead of people, and most days I'd talk you out of needing it. That's the honest position, and I'd rather earn it than just assert it.

In June 2025 the whole argument about multi-agent systems played out in public, fully formed, in a single day. Cognition published "Don't Build Multi-Agents." Anthropic published "How we built our multi-agent research system." One day apart.[^1] Two serious teams, looking at the same technology, came to opposite conclusions in the same week. That tells you the question isn't settled by intelligence or taste. It's settled by what you're doing with it, and the two posts were quietly about different work.

People reach for multiple agents because one model on its own feels limited, and bolting on a second feels like adding a second person to a project: more hands, more throughput. That intuition is wrong in a specific way, and the specific way it's wrong is the whole story.

## Where the cost actually goes

The expensive part of a multi-agent system isn't the models. It's coordination. And coordination isn't a tax you can architect away. It's intrinsic, and it grows faster than the number of actors you add.

Watch how fast. Two actors have one relationship to keep consistent. Add a third and you haven't added one relationship, you've added three. A fourth puts you at six. The work of keeping everyone in agreement climbs faster than the headcount that's supposedly doing the work, because every new actor can interact with every actor already there. You're not buying throughput in proportion to the agents you add, you're buying overhead in proportion to the pairs.

![A complete graph growing from two agents to eight. As each agent is added it has to connect to every agent already there, so the connections, the coordination cost, climb as n(n-1)/2 while the team grows by one.](./figures/coordination-explosion.svg)

*Add one agent. The team grows by one; the connections it has to keep in sync grow by the whole team.*

Here's the asymmetry that runs everything. Reads parallelize, writes don't. Ten agents reading the same document is free, you can have a hundred or a thousand and it costs you nothing but the reading. Two agents writing the same document is a negotiation, and the negotiation is the bill. You've felt this in any system you've ever run. A job waits on another job that's waiting back on it, and nothing moves, you've built a deadlock without ever typing the word. One shared login gets rate-limited and every task that leaned on it fails at the same instant, because they were never really independent, they just looked independent until the thing they shared ran out. A flaky step retries forever and quietly burns money until someone notices and kills it. None of these are model failures. The model was fine. The cost lives in the system around the model, and it scales with how much the actors share, not how smart they are.

Distributed systems named these failures decades ago: the write-write conflict, the lost update where one change quietly buries another. The fixes are algorithmic and they go two ways. You funnel writes through a single leader so one wins and the loser retries, or you let a CRDT fold everyone's writes together by a fixed rule. That second path is mostly closed to us, because a CRDT can only merge mechanical state, and an agent's write is a paragraph of judgment from a non-deterministic writer that no rule fuses while keeping what both sides actually meant. So we fall back on the oldest move there is: keep two writers off the same state, push them through one, and let the reads fan out to everyone.

![A diagram titled "writes do not parallelize": two agents write the same user profile as different shapes, a third agent merges them into a third shape, and the result cascades through incompatible structures, mismatched schemas, and broken endpoints into system failure. A green panel notes that reads do the opposite and combine cleanly.](./figures/write-conflict-cascade.svg)

*The same asymmetry, drawn out. Two agents write the same record as different shapes, a third tries to reconcile them, and the writes cascade into incompatible schemas and broken endpoints. Reads combine cleanly; writes do not.*

Anthropic was blunt about the price. Their multi-agent setups burned roughly fifteen times the tokens of an ordinary chat.[^2] I read that number as the coordination tax made visible. Every agent has to re-establish context the others already have, restate what it's doing, and get told what changed while it was busy. The work the user asked for is a fraction of those tokens. The rest is the actors keeping each other in sync.

I've started calling the long-run version of this agent debt. Not the cost of building the system, the cost of keeping it coherent forever. Every extra loop that can act on shared state is one more thing that can drift, one more way to recreate the two-workers-one-ticket mess, one more reason you wake up to a state nobody intended. You don't pay agent debt once. You pay it every day the thing runs, and it compounds with the number of actors who can write.

You can't out-think a coordination problem. A smarter model won't buy you out of it, because a smarter model doesn't make the writes stop conflicting. The only two moves that genuinely lower the cost are removing actors or removing what they share. Everything else just rearranges the negotiation.

## A better model means fewer agents, not more

This part runs backward from the intuition. The whole reason to split work across several agents was that one of them couldn't hold it all: context too big, task too broad, job too long for a single loop to keep in its head. Multiple agents were a workaround for a limit.

So when the model gets better, the case for multiple agents gets weaker, not stronger. A more capable model holds more, reasons further, stays coherent longer. It dissolves the exact limit that justified splitting the work in the first place. The frontier model doesn't make coordination cheaper, it makes coordination less necessary. Every jump in capability is quietly an argument for fewer agents, because the reason you reached for more of them was scarcity, and scarcity is the thing that just got relieved.

This is why I distrust most of what gets sold as multi-agent. There's a move worth calling agentwashing, where a perfectly good program gets dressed up in the language of agents because that's the language that's funded this year. Worth deflating, and the deflation doubles as a test you can actually run.

Take your system and rename every agent a "function." Strip the names, the roles, the personas, the whole costume. The planner becomes `plan()`, the researcher `research()`, the writer `write()`. Now look at what's left and ask if it still does the same thing. If `plan()` calls `research()`, gets a value back, hands it to `write()`, and you can reason about the whole thing as one program with some slow, fallible subroutines, then you never had a multi-agent system. You had a program in unusual notation. One thing decided what happened next and called out to other things to do bounded, stateless pieces of it, and those other things answered and went away. That's an orchestrator and a pile of tool calls, and it's a perfectly good thing to be. It's what most systems should be, honestly. Programs are things we know how to make correct.

The interesting case is the one where the rename breaks. Where `research()` and `write()` are both running at once, both reaching into the same shared state, neither waiting on the other's return value, each one's behavior depending on what the other did a second ago. That's when you've actually built a multi-agent system, and it's the only place all that coordination cost buys you anything. Everywhere else the costume is the only multi-agent thing about it, and that's good news that just looks like bad news. Programs are far easier to keep correct than negotiations.

And it's not just my experience. When researchers at Berkeley hand-annotated 1,642 failures across seven multi-agent frameworks, the two biggest causes were bad system design and agents talking past each other, and not one of the fourteen failure modes they catalogued was the model reasoning poorly.[^6] The thing you reach for a smarter model to fix isn't even on the list.

## When you actually need them

I owe you the honest version, because the maximal system I built is overkill for almost everyone, me included on most days, and I'd rather say that than sell it.

There's a real band where the extra actors are worth it, and it comes in two shapes worth telling apart. The first is the easy one, and it's what the public numbers are about: work that's mostly reading. When the job is to explore a lot of directions at once and the material is bigger than any single context can hold, you can point a dozen agents at it and let them read in parallel, because reads don't collide. Anthropic's research setup beat their single strongest agent by 90.2% on their internal breadth-first eval.[^3] Simon Willison, skeptical for a year, flipped the day after the dueling posts and scoped his conversion to exactly this: breadth-first, heavily parallel, beyond-one-context work.[^4] The shape is real. It's also the least interesting one, because it works by dodging the hard part. Nobody's writing, so there's nothing to keep in sync.

The second shape is the one worth building for, and it's harder for every reason in the first half of this essay. Some work is genuinely too big and too structured for one mind to hold, and not because the context window is small. It has many interdependent parts, real division of labor, specialization you can't flatten into a single prompt, and it runs longer than any one session. It's the size of an organization, not a task, the kind of thing you'd staff with a team instead of a person. This is where the costume test breaks: rename the agents functions and the program stops making sense, because they aren't subroutines returning values, they're specialists holding context and authority no one of them could hold alone. Here you don't get to dodge coordination, and you shouldn't try. You build structure for it on purpose: roles with their own context and tools, clear ownership of who decides what, an organizing layer whose entire job is handing out work and reconciling the writes so the actors don't trample each other. Cognition, who wrote the original "don't," came back in April 2026 to the same design from the inside: the structured systems that hold up are the ones where writes stay single-threaded and the extra agents add intelligence rather than actions.[^5] The coordination cost is the same superlinear cost I warned you about. The difference is the work can't be done without paying it, so you pay it on purpose, and you get back what a single loop never could: specialists holding far more than one mind ever could, kept coherent by a structure you designed instead of one that emerged by accident. That's the frontier, and most of it is unbuilt, because it's hard.

The discipline is knowing which shape you're in, because almost everyone who thinks they're in the second is in the first, or in neither. You earn the structure by the work being genuinely irreducible, not by how satisfying it is to build. If you can flatten it into one prompt, flatten it. If you can serialize it through one loop, serialize it. You only buy the organization when the work refuses to collapse.

The one place it was genuinely worth it for me was building the thing itself. OpenAcme is partly built and run by agents inside OpenAcme: one writes the code, one manages releases, and a coordinating agent with its own memory tracks what's half-finished and what's waiting on what. The work never sits still, because a bug a user files turns into code, turns into a release, turns into docs, turns into the reply to the person who filed it. I ran it first as one careful loop, and it failed in a way I didn't expect. It wasn't too slow. A single mind holding all of it produced averaged judgment. The context fresh off a furious bug report wrote timid, defensive code; the one deep in a refactor answered users like a compiler. Splitting the work into separate agents, each with its own memory and its own narrow job, was never about throughput. It was about keeping the engineering caution, the release discipline, and the user's voice from collapsing into one bland average. And this is the version a bigger model doesn't dissolve: give one agent ten times the context and it still averages the roles it's holding. The separation has to be real, not just roomy. I couldn't partition the work either, because that one bug report moved through all of it, and the seams between the roles were exactly where the value lived. So each agent got its own identity, workspace, and memory, and the only state they shared was the code and the tracker, which they reached through the coordinating agent, one write at a time. That's the lesson I didn't expect: the moment the work stops being read-only, a shared identity and a shared workspace are exactly where two writers collide.

![Animated diagram of safe multi-agent collaboration: three agents, each with its own identity and workspace, send writes through one coordinator that serializes them one at a time into shared state, while reads flow back to every agent in parallel.](./figures/safe-collaboration.svg)

*How the write-heavy case actually works: each agent keeps its own identity and workspace, writes only its own and reaches shared state as itself, one write at a time through the coordinator, while reads run in parallel. Permissions, not trust, keep the writes from colliding.*

So the engineering forks on which shape you're in. For almost every system, the best work you can do makes the agents behave like one careful actor: serialize the writes, shrink the shared state, let a single mind hold as much as it can and split only what it provably can't. For the rare system that's genuinely the size of an organization, the work is the opposite and a lot harder: building the structure that lets many specialists stay coherent without one mind holding it all. That second problem is the real one, and it's why I build OpenAcme, a self-hosted platform aimed squarely at it.

And when the best version of your design keeps collapsing back toward a single writer, it isn't failing to be multi-agent. It's showing you what it wanted to be all along.
