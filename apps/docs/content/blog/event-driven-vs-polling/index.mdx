One of my agents slept through its own work, and I didn't notice for hours.

Here's what that looked like. A message had come in for it. The thing it was waiting on had finished an hour before, so it was free to go. And it just... didn't. No error, no crash, it just never ran. The work sat there ready and nobody picked it up. The agent was fine, it just never woke up.

Those are the bugs I hate. Not the ones that page you. The ones that don't page you at all, the ones you only find because someone asks why nothing happened.

Quick context, because the lesson has nothing to do with what my agents actually do: I run a bunch of programs that mostly sit idle and now and then have to wake up and do something expensive. In my case each one is backed by an LLM, so a wake is a model call or two, real money and a few real seconds. But mentally swap "agent" for "any slow, costly job that touches state you care about" and nothing changes. A nightly export. A VM that takes a minute to boot. Some rate-limited API you can only poke so often. Same story.

Something has to decide when each of these wakes up. That's the whole post. And the punchline, which took me way too long and an embarrassing amount of deleted code to accept, is that the right answer is a dumb loop on a timer, and I only got there after building the clever version first and watching it fall over.

Let me be straight about the order, because this is easy to read as "guy crawls back to where he started." Not quite. My first version did have a timer, a bad one, and I ripped it out on purpose, because reacting to events the moment they happen is obviously better, right? Message lands, handle it now. Dependency finishes, wake the thing now. No wasted work, every wake traceable to a cause. It's the design you'd sketch on a whiteboard and feel smart about. So the real path was: bad timer, then a clean event-driven thing I was proud of, then back to a timer. It felt like going backwards. It was right anyway.

And to be clear up front, this isn't a case against event-driven in general. Polling is usually the worse option, wasteful, a bit laggy, the thing you're taught to grow out of. This is only about the narrow kind of work where that flips, and it's narrower than the title makes it sound.

*The usual version of this meme ends in something baroque and clever. Mine ends in a loop.*

## The instant-reaction trap

Reacting the instant something happens sounds simple. It isn't, and the reason is kind of dumb: by the time you react, the world has usually moved out from under the event.

The event-driven version grew the way these always grow, one reasonable patch at a time. Signals would arrive in a burst, so I added something to squash a flurry into one wake instead of ten. An agent could get stuck waking itself in a loop, so I added a rate limit. An agent's own actions would echo back as fresh signals and wake it again, so I added a filter to ignore its own echo. Every one of those was a sensible fix to a real problem. And that's the trap. I was so busy patching symptoms that I never stopped to ask whether the thing I kept patching was the actual problem. The growing pile of patches was the answer, and I stared straight at it for months.

The silent failure I opened with wasn't a one-off. There were a handful, and lined up they all rhyme. A signal shows up for an agent whose situation has quietly changed, gets routed nowhere, vanishes, work waits forever. A signal that actually matters gets mistaken for the agent's own echo and dropped, so the one wake I needed is the one the system ate. A wake fires at an agent that's already mid-reply to a person, and now two things are writing to the same place and stomping each other.

That last one you've felt, even if you've never touched a scheduler. You ask some chatbot a question, then fire off a second message before it's done. Now two answers are being written into one conversation. Which wins? In my system the answer was "nobody knows," because nothing decided. They just raced.

And notice what none of these are: none of them is a bug in the actual logic. The rules were fine. Every single one lived in the gap between a signal firing and a busy, expensive agent being ready to deal with it. Races, dropped messages, stale assumptions. You don't get those from your business logic. You get them from reacting.

So I fixed them. All of them. And then I did the thing that should've been a slap in the face: I wrote a watchdog. If an agent woke a few times in a row and found nothing to do, the watchdog would step in and calm it down, because my clever event system could fire an agent at literally nothing, over and over, burning money on calls that did nothing. I had written a program whose entire job was to babysit my scheduler and protect me from it. That's not a feature you ship proudly. That's a confession.

## Sophistication ran backwards

The fix wasn't another patch. It was to stop reacting.

I deleted the whole event-driven path and dropped in a loop. Every sixty seconds it wakes up, walks the agents, asks each one "anything to do here?", and if so, does it. That's the scheduler. That's all of it.

Here's the part that actually matters, so I'll spell it out: the events didn't go away. They stopped being triggers and became data. Before, a signal fired and something reacted right then. Now a signal gets written down in a list and waits, and next time the loop comes around it reads the list and handles whatever's there. Your second chatbot message doesn't race the first anymore, it gets in line and gets picked up in order. Same information. It just went from an alarm going off to a note left on your desk.

And all that machinery? Gone. The burst-squasher, the rate limit, the echo filter, the watchdog, the special cases, the bookkeeping tracking who was owed a wake, all of it deleted, because every piece of it only existed to survive reacting in real time, and I'd stopped reacting in real time. I deleted way more than I added. The thing went from something I couldn't keep in my head to a loop I can describe in one sentence.

The watchdog is the bit I keep thinking about. The thing I'd been quietly proud of just wasn't needed anymore. A loop that runs once a minute physically cannot spin. An idle agent wakes, sees nothing, goes back to sleep, once a minute, harmless. The protection I'd carefully engineered fell out for free the second I stopped reacting.

## The workload was the whole argument

So why does the fashionable answer fall apart here when it's great everywhere else? Because reacting instantly was built for a different kind of work and I dragged it somewhere it didn't belong.

For a web server, reacting the instant a request lands is exactly right. A request is cheap, it brings its own context, there are thousands a second, and the whole game is to answer each one right now, in parallel, in milliseconds. A wasted reaction costs basically nothing, so you optimize for never missing one. Fine. That is just not my world.

My work is the opposite on every axis that counts. It's expensive: a wake is a model call or two, real money and real seconds, so a wasted wake is a line item, not a rounding error. It's one-at-a-time: each agent has one running transcript that every wake writes onto, so fire two at once and you get two replies tangled into one history that makes sense to nobody (that's the chatbot bug, again). And it's in no rush: nobody is watching a spinner for a background agent, so a minute of lag before it starts is invisible. I spent a stupid amount of effort shaving a delay to zero that not one person could perceive.

There's a fourth one and it's the sharpest. Every big model provider caches the front of your prompt now, the standing instructions plus the conversation so far, and if your next call starts with the exact same text they charge you a fraction for that part. Big discount. But it only kicks in when the start of the request is identical to last time. A scheduler that reacts to every little change is constantly rebuilding and resending, so that cached prefix keeps shifting and the discount keeps evaporating. The calm loop leaves things alone between beats, so the prefix stays put and you keep the discount. You already know this shape from a database that's only fast once its cache is warm. Reacting to everything keeps it permanently cold.

And none of those four are about AI, which is the actual point. Wherever they line up, expensive, one writer, no rush, reacting to every change is the wrong reflex, doesn't matter what the work is.

Put it together and there isn't much of a choice left. When each reaction is expensive and a slow one is invisible, you want a few deliberate, well-timed reactions, not a thousand instant ones. Which is the exact opposite of the web request the whole event-driven style was built around. I'd grabbed the wrong tool and then paid for it one bug at a time, for a long time.

The annoying part is that none of this is new. There's a name for it. Reacting the moment something changes is edge-triggered: you catch the instant of change, and if you miss it, it's gone, which is exactly the lost wake I opened with. Checking the current state on a beat is level-triggered: miss a beat and the state is still sitting there next time. And the most trusted infrastructure most of us run all day works the second way. Kubernetes doesn't subscribe to a "a server died" firehose and panic. It wakes on a timer, looks at how things actually are versus how they're supposed to be, and nudges. It's a loop. It's a poll. The thing I was too clever to reach for is the thing the pros already trust.

## The loop has its bills too

I'm not going to pretend the loop is free. It's slower: an agent can take up to a minute to notice new work, unless I nudge it for the rare thing that genuinely can't wait, which I kept as a small explicit escape hatch instead of the whole design. So instant reaction isn't gone, it just got demoted to a narrow seat instead of running the place. The loop also does a little pointless work, an idle agent waking once a minute to confirm there's nothing to do. For my stuff that's completely fine. For yours, maybe not, and that's honestly your call to make, not mine.

But look at the trade. I gave up instant reactions and a whole class of silent, unbounded, expensive failures, and what I got back were failures that are loud, bounded, and cheap. A pointless wake costs nothing and shows up right there on a graph. A lost wake costs you the actual work and shows up nowhere. I'll take the first one every time.

So here's the rule, with my stuff stripped out: when you have to wake something that's expensive, holds state, and isn't in a hurry, don't let the events pull the trigger. Let them pile up. You pull the trigger, on a steady beat, by looking at how things actually are. Keep instant reaction for the handful of cases that truly can't wait, and make them earn it.

The rewrite itself was easy. The hard part was admitting the primitive answer was the right one. A loop on a timer is the thing you write in your first week, and reaching for it after years of supposedly knowing better felt like losing. It wasn't. It's been boring ever since, no watchdog, no special cases, no silent bug I only find out about a week later. And in this job, boring is about the highest praise there is. I just had to outrun the smarter idea to get to it.
